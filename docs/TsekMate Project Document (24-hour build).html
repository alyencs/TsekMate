TsekMate

Every step checked. Every teacher in control.

AI-assisted, step-by-step grading of handwritten student work, with teacher approval and parent-ready feedback. Entry for the AI in Education Hackathon 2026: "Transforming Education Through Artificial Intelligence."

1. Summary

TsekMate reads a photo of handwritten math or science work, checks it step by step against the teacher's rubric, and produces a marked-up draft with feedback. The teacher reviews, edits, and approves it. Only approved results reach the gradebook and the parent update.
Category Teachers: AI Grading Assistant (also touches Teacher Workload Reduction, Parent-School Communication, Learning Analytics)
Primary user Public and private school teachers of math and science (Grades 7 to 10 for the prototype)
Secondary users Students (see approved feedback), parents (see plain-language updates), school heads
AI used Vision-capable LLM (OCR + reasoning), structured output, confidence scoring, NLP summarization and translation
Designed to fit School systems such as Livro's Wela+ / Silid LMS through a clean adapter API (prototype uses a mock; not an official integration)

Build scope: this document describes the full TsekMate design and what we will build in the 24-hour hackathon window. Features are marked Built (the core of the 24-hour build), Stretch (only if the core is stable), or Roadmap (designed, not built). Section 13 has the hour-by-hour plan.

2. The Problem

Teachers are overloaded, and grading handwritten work is a big part of it.

  • Surveys of more than 15,000 teachers across over 2,000 Philippine schools, run by IDInsight with EDCOM 2, found teachers working about 52 hours a week on average, with roughly 17.8 hours on ancillary tasks against the 10 hours DepEd prescribes. Checking assessments and grading performance tasks are among those tasks.
  • Under DepEd Order No. 005, s. 2024, teachers have six hours of classroom teaching and two hours for ancillary work, which includes assessing learner progress. In practice the grading spills into evenings and weekends.
  • DepEd has been cutting paperwork (reducing required teacher forms from 174 to five regular forms), which shows the system recognizes the burden. But grading itself is not something a form reduction removes.

Students wait too long for feedback. When a class of 40 submits worksheets, feedback arrives days later, after the lesson has moved on. Marking only the final answer also hides where the student's reasoning broke down.

Disruptions make it worse. Typhoons, floods, and suspensions interrupt classes. Learning platforms such as Silid LMS already let students work and upload written activities remotely, but the teacher still grades each upload by hand.

The gap: most automated graders handle multiple choice or typed answers. Handwritten, multi-step work is the hardest to automate, which is why it is still done by hand.

3. The Solution

3.1 Teacher flow

  1. Create an activity. Enter the problems, an answer key, and a rubric (for example: setup 2 pts, method 3 pts, computation 3 pts, final answer 2 pts). A rubric template library speeds this up.
  2. Collect work. Photos arrive from the student's upload, or the teacher photographs a stack of papers.
  3. AI drafts the grade. For each submission, TsekMate transcribes the work, splits it into steps, and judges each step against the rubric.
  4. Teacher reviews. The review screen shows the photo beside the transcript, a verdict per step (correct, error, unclear), an error type, a confidence score, and a suggested score. Low-confidence items are flagged first.
  5. Teacher approves or edits. Nothing is recorded until the teacher approves.
  6. Outputs. The approved score goes to the gradebook. The student sees feedback. A parent update is drafted in English, Filipino, or Bisaya and also needs teacher approval.
  7. Class summary. TsekMate shows the most common errors across the class (for example, "14 of 38 students distributed the negative sign incorrectly") and suggests a short reteach focus.

In the 24-hour prototype, work enters by photo upload from the teacher's phone or laptop (no student upload portal), and the gradebook is a mock page.

3.3 What TsekMate is not

  • It does not solve homework for students or generate answers to unsubmitted work. It evaluates work a student has already done.
  • It does not make final grading decisions. Teachers do.
  • By default, student feedback points to the error and gives a hint instead of printing the full solution, and teachers can change this setting.

4. Why AI Is Needed

A rules-based system can check a typed final answer against a key. It cannot:

  • Read handwriting, including messy, slanted, or smudged writing, plus math notation (fractions, exponents, symbols).
  • Follow a student's reasoning across several steps and recognize a valid alternative method.
  • Explain why a step is wrong in plain language and in context.
  • Group similar mistakes across a class without a hand-built rule for each one.

That requires vision (to read) and language reasoning (to judge and explain), which is why a vision-capable LLM is central. To keep it reliable, TsekMate pairs the LLM with deterministic checks (see Section 6.4).

5. Research Basis

5.1 What current research says about this approach

  • It works well with good rubrics, but reading handwriting is the weak point. A 2026 evaluation of vision-capable LLMs grading authentic handwritten math from two university STEM courses (Levine et al., arXiv 2605.19043) reported that the best cost-effective model reached about 95% accuracy on individual rubric items when rubrics were well designed. In the best model, around 87% of its errors came from transcription failures rather than misapplying the rubric. An earlier pipeline using GPT-4V plus MathPix reached only about 60%. Design lesson: invest in image quality, clear rubrics, and flagging unreadable work.
  • Models can penalize the wrong thing. A study of multimodal LLMs on handwritten work (arXiv 2510.05538) found that vision accuracy is a strong predictor of grading accuracy, that some models marked down a faulty process even when the final answer was right, and that some produced more false negatives than others. Design lesson: grade steps against an explicit rubric, test more than one model, and keep the teacher in the loop.
  • Grading incorrect work is harder than grading correct work. An evaluation of GPT-4o on handwritten college math exams (arXiv 2411.05231) found predicted scores were still off by about 7.7% on average, and the model struggled more on incorrect solutions. Design lesson: weaker students' work needs the most teacher attention, so route it to review.
  • Error types matter. The FERMAT benchmark (arXiv 2501.07244) evaluates vision-language models on 2,244 handwritten school-level math solutions across four error axes: computational, conceptual, notation, and presentation. TsekMate adopts the same four categories for tagging.
  • Humans disagree too. A study of vision-language models on handwritten exam grading (arXiv 2609.14284) found that agreement between two faculty markers was lower than the agreement between the best tuned model and the examiner it was calibrated to. This does not mean the AI is "better." It means grading consistency is a real issue and an AI second opinion with transparent reasoning can help, as long as teachers decide.

What we claim: TsekMate is a draft-and-review assistant. We do not claim it replaces teachers or matches them on every paper.

6.3 Data model (core tables)

24-hour build: students (pseudonymous ID, section), activities, rubrics (criteria, points), submissions (image reference, status), ai_results (steps JSON, suggested score, confidence, model, prompt version), teacher_reviews (final score, edits, timestamp).

Later: parent_messages (language, draft, approved flag) as a stretch item, and a full audit_log (who did what, when).

6.4 How we make the AI reliable

  • Single-call transcribe-and-grade (Built), with structured JSON output validated against a schema. (The Levine et al. study cites earlier findings that a single call outperformed separating transcription from grading.)
  • Explicit rubric in the prompt (Built), with the teacher's key and sample solutions.
  • Confidence routing (Built): unclear transcription or low confidence goes to the top of the teacher's queue.
  • Prompt and model versioning (Built): each result stores the model and prompt version.
  • Deterministic cross-check (Stretch): for algebra, SymPy tests whether each step follows from the previous one. Disagreement with the LLM lowers confidence and flags the item.
  • Image quality gate (Stretch): blurry or dark photos get a retake prompt before they reach the model. In the build, we use clear photos and a retake note in the UI.

6.5 Integration with school systems (bonus criterion)

Schools already run platforms like Wela+ (Silid LMS, grading, parent app). TsekMate is designed as a separate AI service behind an adapter:

  • Adapter endpoints: GET /activities, GET /submissions (pull work), POST /grades/draft (push approved scores), POST /notifications (parent updates). We define these in the document and the API spec.
  • The prototype implements a mock gradebook page with synthetic data. Swapping in a real API means replacing the adapter, not rebuilding the service.
  • We describe TsekMate as designed to plug into such platforms. We do not claim an existing partnership or access to any vendor's real APIs or data.

7. Data Privacy and Responsible AI

7.1 Why this matters

Under the Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173), information about a person's education, including grades and school records, is sensitive personal information. National Privacy Commission guidance notes that learners' privacy rights are exercised by parents or guardians when students are minors, and that schools remain responsible for data even when they outsource processing. Student handwriting also identifies a person. (We are not lawyers, and this document is not legal advice.)

7.3 Responsible AI

  • Bias and fairness: we test a small set across handwriting styles (neat, cursive, slanted, cramped) and report results per style. With about 15 samples, these are indicative only. Weak groups get more review prompts, and we say so openly.
  • Hallucination handling: structured output, rubric grounding, SymPy cross-checks, confidence flags, and mandatory review.
  • Accountability: the prototype stores AI output, teacher edits, and final decisions, with the model and prompt version.
  • Translation quality: Filipino parent messages (stretch) and any later Bisaya messages are machine drafts and are labeled as needing teacher or native-speaker review before sending.
  • Limits stated openly: poor handwriting, unusual notation, and diagrams lower accuracy. TsekMate is a decision-support tool.

10. Answers to the Judges' Four Questions

  1. What educational problem does it solve? Slow, labor-intensive grading of handwritten work, which delays student feedback and adds to teacher overload.
  2. Why is AI needed? Reading handwriting and judging multi-step reasoning cannot be done with fixed rules.
  3. Who benefits? Teachers (time), students (faster, specific feedback), parents (clear updates), and school heads (class-wide insight).
  4. Can schools actually use it? Yes. It works from a phone camera, keeps the teacher in control, and is designed to connect to existing school platforms through an adapter.

14. Demo and Presentation Outline (about 5 minutes)

  1. Hook (30 s): "It's 9 PM. Ma'am is still checking 40 notebooks, and students will see feedback on Thursday."
  2. Problem and evidence (30 s): workload figures, delayed feedback, typhoon disruption.
  3. Live demo (2 min): photograph a messy handwritten solution, see step-by-step marking, a flagged low-confidence step, the teacher edits one score and approves, and the grade appears in the mock gradebook. If the stretch item is done, the parent message appears in Filipino.
  4. Class view (30 s): the most common error across our sample set.
  5. How the AI works and the technical stack (45 s): the architecture diagram and the reliability layers.
  6. Privacy and responsible AI (30 s): synthetic data, redaction, teacher approval, audit log.
  7. Results and next steps (30 s): measured accuracy on our sample set, what we did not get to, and the roadmap.
